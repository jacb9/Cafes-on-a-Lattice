Cafés on a Lattice

Two cafés three blocks apart fight over the same morning customers. Two cafés at opposite ends of town never share a customer, but they may well be trying to hire the same barista. This model puts both kinds of competition on one grid. Customer competition is local, and you set how far it reaches. Labour competition is regional: every café hires from one pool. The question for the seminar is which mechanism decides how many cafés survive, and which decides where they sit.

Specification

How the model works

Each square is a city block with ten potential customers per period. A square holds one café or none. Two forces act on every café in every period, and they act at different spatial scales. That difference is the point of the model.

Customers are local. A customer considers only cafés whose square lies within the reach R (squares whose centres sit within R + ½ of their own). Each café in reach pulls with a weight that falls with distance d, scaled by the café's own attractiveness a, drawn between 0.6 and 1.4 when it opens. Staying home has a fixed weight of 0.5, so customers far from any café buy nothing. Each option gets its weight's share of the customers.

weight = a · (1 + d)−β    sharei = weighti / (0.5 + Σ weights in reach)

Labour is not local. All cafés hire from one regional pool. A café needs one worker per 20 customers. When cafés jointly want more workers than the pool holds, two things happen. The wage moves halfway toward a target that rises with labour demand, and staffing is rationed, so every café serves proportionally fewer of the customers it attracted. A café in one corner is squeezed by hiring in the other corner, though the two never share a customer.

target wage = 8 · (workers wanted / pool)η    profit = (1 − wage/20) · customers served − F

Entry and exit. A café that loses money for as many consecutive periods as its patience allows closes. Each empty square has a chance, set by the entry rate, of attracting a prospective entrant. The entrant estimates what an average café would earn there given current rivals and the current wage, and opens with a probability that rises with that estimate. All squares update at once, and the map wraps at its edges, so no café enjoys a protected corner.

For discussion

Questions for the seminar

  • Raise reach from 2 to 8 and hold everything else fixed. What happens to the number of cafés, and to the spacing index? Is either relationship linear? Then run the sweep over R and check whether your eyeball impression survives replication.
  • Use the Tight labour market scenario. Density falls, but does the spatial pattern change? Try to name which outcomes belong to the local mechanism and which to the non-local one. Some outcomes may belong to both, which is a harder thing to show.
  • Run one setting under five different seeds. How much of what you saw in the first run is still there? This is the practical argument for replications before interpreting a simulation.
  • Pause at period 50, then continue to 1000. Which period is the result? Decide on a burn-in window before you look at the output, not after. The sweep uses the last quarter of each run; is that defensible for 100 periods?
  • Change reach mid-run. The system was in some kind of balance, and now it is not. How long does it take to settle again, and does it settle where a fresh run with the new reach would?

What the model leaves out

Prices are fixed and identical. Cafés cannot choose quality, hours or size. Workers have no geography of their own, so commuting costs do not exist and one wage holds everywhere. Customers do not move or learn. Each of these is a modelling decision, and each could be relaxed. Before you trust a pattern from this grid, ask which of these omissions it depends on.
